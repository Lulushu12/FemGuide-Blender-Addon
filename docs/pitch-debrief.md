# FemGuide — Pitch Debrief

Notes from two mock discovery calls: an implant OEM product manager and a
high-volume knee surgeon. Written as working material for real calls, not as a
record of the mock.

Product context: FemGuide is planning/design software producing a one-piece
patient-specific femoral cutting guide plus a tibial guide, sold with on-site
consulting and optional printing. Market: Romania / EU. Current status at time
of these calls: 25 clinical cases under MDR Annex XIII, post-op CT on all,
OSIM patent filed with PCT filed, ISO 13485 not yet in place.

---

## 1. The headline

Both buyers ended the call still interested. Neither of them bought what you
were selling — they bought two different things, and neither was accuracy.

- **The product manager bought logistics.** Four trays down to two printed parts
  is a capital and reprocessing line he can defend to a board. Accuracy was his
  *permission to consider it*, not his reason.
- **The surgeon bought honesty and zero added workload.** The two answers that
  moved them were "keep the conventional trays open until you trust me" and
  "you don't plan anything, I do." Neither is a product feature.

Accuracy is the entry ticket. It is not the pitch. Reposition accordingly.

---

## 2. The habit that cost you the most: you bury the lede

Twice, the single strongest fact you own arrived as a subordinate clause, deep
in an answer about something else.

| Asset | Where it surfaced | Where it belonged |
|---|---|---|
| 25 clinical cases | Third exchange of the OEM call, mid-paragraph, after saw kerf | First sentence of the call |
| Planned-vs-achieved within 0.5° on post-op CT | Fourth exchange of the surgeon call, inside an answer about cartilage | Second sentence of the call |

Both listeners had to stop you and drag it out. In a real call nobody stops
you — they let you talk until the time runs out, then say they'll be in touch.

**Open every call with:** *"Twenty-five consecutive primary TKAs, one-piece
femoral guide, post-op CT on every case, planned versus achieved measured on
all of them. That's what I'm here to talk about."* Then let them choose which
thread to pull.

## 3. Where you would have lost the room

Four moments. Two per call.

### 3.1 Defending an error by normalising it (OEM call — most damaging)

You were asked what happens when a cut leaves the femoral box 2 mm proud. You
answered that it happens all the time with worn conventional guides, that
surgeons re-cut freehand, and that cement covers minor conformation issues.

That answer destroys your own value proposition. If 2 mm doesn't matter and is
routinely corrected freehand, the buyer has no reason to pay for accuracy. You
argued yourself out of the product in three sentences.

**Never defend your error budget by arguing that errors are normal.** Defend it
with your measured distribution.

### 3.2 "Public knowledge, and I'm not profiting from it" (OEM call)

Three problems, in ascending order of seriousness:

1. You *are* profiting — you charge for design and for printing.
2. Sizing tables give nominal dimensions. They do not give manufacturing
   tolerances, cement mantle allowance, box radii, or peg geometry. You have a
   database of approximations of someone's implant, and you cut bone against it.
3. Under MDR, a guide dimensioned to place a *specific* manufacturer's component
   is arguably an accessory to that component, and you are making a
   compatibility claim about a device you have never been given data for.

The argument is also strategically unnecessary. The correct framing is not
"I'm allowed to" — it is *"I'd rather have your CAD under an agreement than my
best inference without one."* That reframe turns your weakest point into the
single best reason for the OEM to be in the conversation at all.

### 3.3 Dismissing cartilage to a knee surgeon (surgeon call)

"Cartilage is sparse in such knees" is true medially in a varus knee and false
laterally, where 2–3 mm often remains. That asymmetry is precisely the classic
PSI seating error: the guide doesn't rock, it *tilts*, sits stably while tilted,
and gets pinned.

You compounded it by then stating that the guide's largest registration surface
is the distal condyles — which is where the cartilage is. You dismissed the
problem and registered on it in consecutive answers.

You also have the strongest possible rebuttal available and didn't use it: 25
cases with post-op CT agreeing with plan. Lead with the data, concede the
mechanism.

### 3.4 Answering the question you preferred (surgeon call)

Asked "what's on my back table when your guide doesn't fit," you answered a
question about liability and planning responsibility. Surgeons notice dodges
instantly and read them as evasion even when they're just disorganisation.

When you don't have the answer yet, say so: *"I don't have a good answer to
that. Here's what I do have."* That costs you nothing and buys you credibility
you can't purchase any other way.

**Honourable mention:** *"We applied for ISO and are waiting for an answer."*
You don't apply for ISO 13485. You implement a QMS — document control, design
history file, ISO 14971 risk management, supplier controls, CAPA, post-market
surveillance — and a certification body audits what you built. The certificate
is a receipt for completed work. The phrasing signals you may not know that.

## 4. Two contradictions between the calls

You will eventually pitch an OEM and a surgeon who talk to each other. These
need reconciling before that happens.

### C1 — Is your error rate zero, or is error routine?

- To the PM: 2 mm proud boxes happen all the time and cement absorbs them.
- To the surgeon: it never happens, a malrotated box never happens, it will
  always be a perfect cut.

Both are the same reflex — defending by assertion rather than by distribution.
Replace both with one sentence containing a mean, an SD, and a worst case.

### C2 — Do the trays go away, or stay on the back table?

- To the PM: two printed parts replace four trays. This was the pitch he
  actually responded to.
- To the surgeon: keep the conventional trays open until trust is earned.

Both answers were individually correct. Together they mean **the OEM's business
case does not materialise in year one.** Worse, trays don't leave a hospital
because one surgeon stops opening them — they're stocked under the system
contract for the whole department.

Say the reconciled version to the OEM *before* he hears it from a surgeon:
> "Year one is additive cost — the trays stay open. Tray elimination is a
> departmental conversion and it's a year-two-onwards outcome, contingent on a
> department converting, not a surgeon. What I can show you in year one is the
> accuracy and OR-time data that makes the year-two conversation possible."

That answer loses you nothing and it is the reason he'll trust the rest of
your numbers.

### C3 (minor) — "any implant on the market"

Your flexion-gap answer assumed posterior referencing. On an anterior-referencing
system, downsizing the femur *does* take more posterior condyle and *is* how a
tight flexion gap gets opened. Matching a component's dimensions is not the same
as reproducing its surgical technique and referencing philosophy. Narrow the
claim to what you've actually validated, and know the surgeon's referencing
philosophy before telling them what their instruments can't do.

## 5. Rewritten answers

Use these close to verbatim.

### 5.1 The opener (30 seconds)

> "Twenty-five consecutive primary knees with a one-piece patient-specific
> femoral guide. Post-op CT on every case, planned versus achieved measured on
> all twenty-five. CT to theatre inside a day. Two printed parts and a tibial
> reamer instead of the full instrument set."

Then split by audience:

- **OEM:** "The part I want to talk to you about is the instrument set."
- **Surgeon:** "The part I want to talk to you about is that it doesn't add a
  single minute to your day."

### 5.2 Accuracy — replacing "under half a degree"

Half a degree is *below the noise floor of the measurement method*. Post-op CT
alignment assessment carries roughly 0.5–1° of measurement error, and
inter-observer variability alone consumes your entire claim. A knowledgeable
listener hears either "he doesn't know how noisy his measurement is" or "he
graded his own homework."

> "Twenty-five cases. Post-op CT on all of them, measured against plan by
> [name/role], who was blinded to the planned values. Distal femoral valgus
> deviated by a mean of X° with an SD of Y. Tibial slope, X° ± Y. Femoral
> rotation, X° ± Y. Worst single case was Z°. Cases outside three degrees: n."

If the measurer wasn't blinded, say so unprompted. A stated limitation buys more
credibility than a spectacular number. **Understated and verifiable beats
spectacular and doubted.**

### 5.3 Error and tolerance — replacing "it never happens"

> "My designed tolerance is ±X mm on translation and ±Y° on the cut planes.
> Across twenty-five cases my measured worst deviation was Z. I've had no cases
> requiring an unplanned re-cut. If it does happen, the failure mode is
> [seating/pin/print], the surgeon detects it at [verification step], and the
> bailout is conventional instrumentation."

Have a stated failure mode. A founder with no known failure mode reads as a
founder who hasn't looked.

### 5.4 Cartilage and CT — the honest version

> "You're right that CT doesn't see cartilage, and you're right that the
> asymmetric case is the risk — bone-on-bone medially, preserved laterally.
> Here's how I handle it: [cartilage thickness assumption / MRI option /
> registration strategy]. And here's the empirical answer: twenty-five cases
> including [n] with a preserved lateral compartment, post-op CT agreeing with
> plan to [X]. If you want, we do your first three on MRI and compare."

Concede the mechanism, answer with the data, offer a test. Never dismiss a
failure mode a surgeon has personally seen.

### 5.5 The back table — keep this one exactly as it was

> "I can't guarantee you anything. Trust needs to be earned. Keep the
> conventional trays open, open them without apology if you don't like the fit,
> and after five cases decide for yourself."

This was the strongest thing said in either call. Do not upgrade it into a
promise.

### 5.6 Implant geometry — replacing "public knowledge"

> "Today I derive component geometry from published sizing tables and technique
> guides — which gives me nominal dimensions, not your tolerances, radii, or peg
> geometry. That's a limitation I'd rather not have. The reason I'm in this room
> is that your CAD under an agreement removes it, for both of us."

## 6. What each buyer actually wants

| | Implant OEM PM | High-volume surgeon |
|---|---|---|
| Buys | Instrument capex, reprocessing cost, loaner logistics | Trust, no added workload, no cancelled lists |
| Ignores | Surgical elegance, alignment philosophy | MDR, patents, QMS, tray capex |
| Kills the deal | Regulatory exposure, IP exposure, unfundable clinical promise | Added minutes, added steps, one bad case, an unanswered phone |
| Proof they accept | Blinded data, a notified body opinion, an FTO letter | A peer's phone number |
| Realistic first ask | Development & evaluation agreement, 6–9 months, CAD under NDA | Three cases, their patient selection, trays open |

**The sequencing insight:** the surgeon asked for referenceable surgeons; the PM
asked for data he could put in a clinical evaluation. Surgeon adoption generates
exactly the evidence that makes the OEM conversation fundable. Run surgeons
first. The OEM conversation is worth several times more with 50 cases and a
published accuracy paper than it is today.

## 7. Asset gaps, in priority order

1. **Write up the accuracy data properly.** Mean, SD, worst case, % outside 3°,
   named measurer, blinded or explicitly not. The scans are already on PACS.
   One week of somebody's time. Highest return of anything on this list, and it
   is the gating item for both buyers.
2. **Establish who the legal manufacturer was on the 25 Annex XIII statements.**
   If the statements name the hospital, the clinical experience belongs to the
   hospital, not to you. Founders discover this during due diligence. Check now.
3. **Freedom-to-operate opinion from patent counsel.** A week of keyword
   searching is not FTO. The risk isn't someone else patenting a one-piece
   guide; it's a broad 2008–2015 claim on patient-specific registration with
   fixation pins reading onto your device without ever using your words. Budget
   €8–15k.
4. **Software regulatory classification, in writing.** FemGuide computes bone
   resection planes. MDR Rule 11 puts it at Class IIa minimum, plausibly IIb.
   Annex XIII covers the *guide* — it is a per-patient, no-CE-mark,
   no-performance-claims route, and it does not reach the software you say you
   are selling. Your product and your regulatory pathway are currently two
   different things. Get a written classification opinion before pricing a
   licence.
5. **QMS reality.** ISO 13485 is a 9–12 month build, €30–60k with a consultant,
   and it gates everything downstream. Know whether it's started.
6. **A seating-verification feature in the guide design.** Both calls asked what
   tells the *surgeon* the guide is correctly seated, and both times the answer
   was about the segmentation. The software's confidence is not available to
   someone holding the part. Add a physical check — a witness surface that
   visibly gaps when tilted, a verification stylus, a confirmation hole checked
   against a landmark. This is a design task and it is also a strong answer to
   the cartilage objection.
7. **Sterilisation.** Validated cycle for the resin at 134°C, a certificate the
   hospital CSSD can file, and a documented answer for the morning a print
   fails with a patient anaesthetised.
8. **Validate the gap simulator.** CT contains no ligaments. Compare simulated
   gaps against what the surgeon actually measured with a tensor in the cases
   you have. If it correlates, that's a genuinely novel claim. If it's
   unvalidated, don't show it.
9. **Address the scale bottleneck.** You do the planning personally. One surgeon
   at 300 cases/year saturates you. State your capacity plan before someone
   works it out for you — a cancelled list costs a hospital more than a year of
   your fees.
10. **Two referenceable surgeons.** Surgeons believe forty seconds from a peer
    over any deck. Ask the two who ran your 25 cases for permission now.

## 8. Questions you will get every time — have the answer ready

- Under what regulatory basis were the cases done, and who was the legal
  manufacturer?
- Planned versus achieved: mean, SD, worst case, who measured, blinded?
- Where does the guide register, and what tells the surgeon in the room that it
  seated correctly?
- What's the bailout, and does the hospital still stock conventional trays?
- Who plans it, how long does it take the surgeon, and what are they signing?
- What's the software's MDR class, and who holds the CE mark?
- Patent: office, priority date, PCT filed, FTO opinion by whom?
- Who sterilises it, when, and what happens if the print fails?
- What's your capacity when I send you twenty knees in a month?
- Which two surgeons can I call?

Ten questions. If all ten have a one-paragraph answer and a document behind
them, the meetings stop being interrogations.

## 9. What actually went well

Worth stating, because the sections above are all corrections.

- **The flexion-gap answer** — posterior referencing, downsizing takes anteriorly
  and doesn't change the posterior condylar resection, PCL and capsular release
  as the route to a tight flexion gap. Technically correct and most people
  selling into this space couldn't produce it. Domain fluency is your credibility.
- **The osteophyte answer** — landmark for placement, pins for fixation, don't
  care if they crumble afterwards. Honest, mechanically sensible, and it changed
  the surgeon's posture.
- **The back-table answer.** Best sixty seconds of either call.
- **Removing the surgeon's planning burden.** Correct instinct. Refine it: give
  the numbers *and* a 30-second render by default, because a rubber stamp is the
  worst of both worlds when the surgeon carries all the legal risk.
- **You conceded the bone-cutting-time wording immediately** when pushed. Fast,
  clean correction without defensiveness reads as competence. Do that every time.
